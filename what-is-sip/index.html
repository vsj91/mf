What Is SIP? A Practical Guide to Systematic Investment Plans

A Systematic Investment Plan (SIP) is a way to invest a chosen amount at regular intervals. The useful question is not only “what does SIP stand for?” but what actually happens to your money each month, how NAV changes the units you receive, and what SIP can and cannot solve.

Written by: Vinay S Jain, creator of PlanSIP   •   Last reviewed: 18 August 2026

SIP is a method, not an investment product

SIP describes how money is invested. The underlying mutual fund determines the actual asset exposure and risk. A ₹5,000 SIP into an equity fund and a ₹5,000 SIP into a liquid fund use the same payment method but have very different risk.

What happens every month

Suppose you invest ₹6,000. At NAV ₹20 you receive about 300 units. If next month NAV falls to ₹15, the same ₹6,000 buys about 400 units. If NAV later rises to ₹24, the value of all accumulated units changes with that NAV. This illustrates why SIP automatically buys more units when NAV is lower, but it does not guarantee profit.

A PlanSIP example: contribution versus assumption

A ₹10,000 monthly SIP for 10 years means ₹12 lakh of contributions before considering returns. A calculator may show a larger future value at an assumed 10% or 12%, but that extra amount is a model result—not money promised by the fund.

Where SIP helps

  • Turns investing into a repeatable habit.
  • Reduces dependence on choosing one perfect entry date.
  • Makes cash-flow planning easier for salaried investors.
  • Allows step-up contributions when income rises.

Where SIP does not help

SIP does not remove equity risk, prevent drawdowns, select a suitable fund for you, or guarantee that a goal will be reached. A short time horizon can still make an equity SIP unsuitable even if payments are monthly.

A better way to decide SIP amount

Start from the goal, time available and realistic contribution capacity. Keep emergency money and near-term obligations separate. Then test multiple return assumptions instead of choosing the highest number because it produces the most attractive corpus.

Common mistake: stopping only because markets fall

Market falls can be uncomfortable. The right response depends on whether your goal, fund suitability and personal circumstances have changed. A fall alone is not proof that a fund is bad; equally, “continue forever” is not a universal rule.

Questions people often ask

Is SIP guaranteed?

No. SIP only schedules investments. Returns depend on the underlying investment and market conditions.

Is a lower NAV better for starting a SIP?

No. NAV level by itself does not tell you whether a fund is cheap or attractive.

Can I increase my SIP later?

Many investors use step-up SIPs or simply increase the contribution periodically, subject to the platform and scheme process.
