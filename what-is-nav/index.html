NAV explained

What is NAV?

NAV stands for Net Asset Value. In mutual funds, NAV represents the per-unit value of a mutual fund scheme. It is calculated from the value of the fund's assets after accounting for liabilities and expenses.

NAV meaning in simple words

NAV means Net Asset Value. It is the value of one unit of a mutual fund scheme.

Mutual funds hold assets such as shares, bonds, cash and other investments. After subtracting liabilities and expenses, the remaining value is divided among the outstanding mutual fund units.

What is the full form of NAV?

The full form of NAV is Net Asset Value.

NAV is commonly used to show the per-unit value of a mutual fund scheme.

What is NAV in mutual funds?

NAV in mutual funds represents the value of the fund's net assets on a per-unit basis.

It changes as the value of the fund's underlying investments, expenses and liabilities change.

How is NAV calculated?

A simplified NAV formula is:

NAV = (Total Assets − Total Liabilities) ÷ Total Outstanding Units

For example, suppose a mutual fund has ₹110 crore of assets, ₹10 crore of liabilities and 2 crore outstanding units.

Net assets: ₹110 crore − ₹10 crore = ₹100 crore

NAV: ₹100 crore ÷ 2 crore units = ₹50 per unit

How NAV affects your investment value

Your mutual fund investment value can be understood using:

Units owned × Current NAV = Approximate investment value

For example, if you own 200 units and the NAV is ₹50:

200 × ₹50 = ₹10,000

If the NAV later rises to ₹55, the same 200 units would be worth approximately ₹11,000.

How NAV works with SIP

When you invest through a Systematic Investment Plan (SIP), each instalment purchases units based on the applicable NAV.

Suppose your SIP amount is ₹5,000.

At NAV ₹50: ₹5,000 ÷ ₹50 = 100 units

At NAV ₹40: ₹5,000 ÷ ₹40 = 125 units

Therefore, the same SIP amount buys more units when NAV is lower and fewer units when NAV is higher.

Does lower NAV mean a mutual fund is cheaper?

No. A lower NAV does not automatically mean that a mutual fund is cheaper, undervalued or likely to generate higher returns.

For example:

Fund A NAV = ₹20
Fund B NAV = ₹200

If both funds increase by 10%, Fund A becomes ₹22 and Fund B becomes ₹220.

Both have generated the same percentage return.

Is higher NAV bad?

No. A high NAV does not mean a mutual fund is expensive or has less growth potential.

NAV mainly reflects the accumulated per-unit value of the scheme. Investors should not choose a mutual fund based only on whether its NAV is high or low.

NAV vs stock price

Mutual fund NAV and stock market prices are different concepts.

A stock price is determined through buying and selling in the stock market. Mutual fund NAV represents the calculated per-unit value of the mutual fund's net assets.

Therefore, a ₹20 mutual fund NAV should not be interpreted in the same way as a ₹20 share price.

Does NAV change every day?

NAV changes as the value of the investments held by the mutual fund changes.

If the value of the underlying portfolio rises, NAV may increase. If portfolio values fall, NAV may decline.

NAV and mutual fund returns

NAV changes can be used to calculate mutual fund returns over a particular period.

For example, if NAV rises from ₹100 to ₹110, the increase is approximately 10%, ignoring distributions and other factors in this simplified example.

For longer periods, measures such as CAGR and, for periodic investments such as SIPs, XIRR may provide more useful return information.

What should you compare instead of NAV?

When studying mutual funds, NAV alone should not be used to decide whether a scheme is attractive.

Consider factors such as:

  • Mutual fund category
  • Investment objective
  • Official Risk-o-Meter
  • Historical returns
  • Volatility and drawdown
  • Expense ratio
  • Exit load
  • Portfolio composition
  • Your investment horizon
