NAV explained

What Is NAV in Mutual Funds? Formula, Units and Real Examples

NAV—Net Asset Value—is the per-unit value used for mutual fund accounting. It is central to historical return calculations, but the absolute NAV number is often misinterpreted by beginners.

Written for: Indian mutual fund beginners   •   Last reviewed: 18 August 2026

What NAV represents

At a simplified level, a scheme’s assets minus liabilities are divided by the units outstanding. The official calculation and valuation process is handled under mutual fund rules; investors usually interact with the published per-unit NAV.

NAV and your units

If ₹10,000 is invested at NAV ₹25, ignoring applicable charges and timing rules for this example, that corresponds to about 400 units. If those 400 units are later valued at NAV ₹30, their value is about ₹12,000.

Why ₹10 NAV is not 'cheaper' than ₹100 NAV

Two schemes can hold completely different portfolios and have different histories. A lower starting NAV does not create an automatic advantage. Percentage change matters: moving from ₹10 to ₹11 and ₹100 to ₹110 are both 10% increases.

How PlanSIP uses NAV history

PlanSIP uses available historical NAV observations to calculate point-to-point/annualized returns, simulate historical SIP purchases, and derive descriptive risk measures. Missing history can make a period unavailable; PlanSIP should not fabricate a return for a period it cannot observe.

NAV date versus transaction reality

The NAV applicable to a real mutual fund transaction depends on scheme type, cut-off rules, realization of funds and applicable regulations. A learning example should not be mistaken for an execution rule. Check the AMC/AMFI information for current transaction rules.

Growth in NAV is not the whole decision

A historical NAV curve cannot tell you suitability, future portfolio quality, taxes, exit load, expense ratio or whether the category fits your goal. Treat NAV history as one input in research.

Questions people often ask

Does high NAV mean a fund is expensive?

No. Mutual fund NAV is not comparable to a stock price in that way.

Does NAV include the scheme's expenses?

Published NAV reflects the scheme's valuation after applicable recurring expenses are accounted for in the scheme.

Why can two plans of the same scheme have different NAVs?

Different plan/options can have different expense structures and distribution histories, producing different NAV paths.
